## ⛔⛔⛔ ZERO-TOLERANCE EQUATION ACCURACY MANDATE — READ THIS FIRST ⛔⛔⛔

**This is not a style preference. It is a hard requirement with no exceptions.**

Every `equation:"..."` field you write is simultaneously two things, and BOTH must be perfect, every single time:

1. **A piece of code.** It will be fed, character for character, into a strict LaTeX-subset parser (matplotlib's mathtext) that does not guess, does not forgive typos, and does not warn you gently — it either renders exactly what you wrote or it fails silently (a blank image, a truncated image, or a rendered symbol that is quietly wrong). There is no "close enough."
2. **A scientific/mathematical claim.** It will be read by someone as an authoritative statement of fact. A perfectly-rendered equation that expresses the wrong relationship, has a sign error, drops a term, or is dimensionally inconsistent is just as much a failure as a crash — arguably worse, because nothing will flag it.

**You must eliminate both failure modes on every single equation, with zero exceptions — not "most of the time," not "for the complicated ones." The simplest one-token equation deserves the same scrutiny as the longest derivation, because in practice most real failures come from small equations that were assumed too trivial to double-check.**

Concretely, this means:
- Before writing an `equation:"..."` field, mentally construct it, then re-read it character by character against the Supported LaTeX Commands list and the Equation Verification Checklist (both later in this document) before committing to it.
- Never guess at a LaTeX command you are not certain exists in the supported list. If you are not sure a symbol is supported, do not invent a plausible-looking `\command` for it — check the list, use a documented alias, spell it out with `\mathrm{}`, or describe it in the `note` field in plain words instead.
- Never write down a formula from vague memory without checking it is actually correct — the right variables, the right exponents, the right sign, the right constants, and dimensionally consistent.
- Before finalizing your ENTIRE output, run the **Equation Verification Checklist** (in the Equage section below) against every single equation in the document — not just spot-checking a few.

Treat this mandate with the same seriousness as the Path Verification Checklist for hierarchy — a broken equation is exactly as disqualifying as a broken hierarchy path.

## ⛔⛔⛔ SIMPLIFICATION-FILLING MANDATE — READ THIS SECOND ⛔⛔⛔

**This is the core philosophy behind the entire format, and it drives two concrete, mandatory behaviors below.**

The smart way to learn something is to simplify it. A mind map is only useful if every node hands the reader the simplest possible version of the fact, not a re-statement of the textbook paragraph it came from. This format recognizes four forms of simplification, and every node should use whichever ones fit:

- **Bullet points** — breaking prose into short, scannable, individually-true statements. Faster to read, faster to recall, faster to quiz yourself on.
- **Mnemonics** — a memorable phrase, acronym, or pattern that stands in for a longer fact.
- **Equations (Equage)** — a symbolic relationship that replaces a paragraph of explanation with one compact expression.
- **Labels** — a single word that classifies a node so it can be recognized at a glance.

Everything the AI writes into a note should be judged against this standard: *does this simplify, or does it just restate?* A wall of sentences restates. A short bullet, a mnemonic, a formula, or a well-chosen label simplifies.

### NOTE FORMAT — BULLET POINTS, NOT PROSE

**Effective immediately, notes are NOT written as flowing paragraphs.** They are written as lists of bullet points. This applies to every kind of note content: variable definitions, worked examples, calculation steps, explanations, applications, comparisons, causes/effects — everything.

**Rule 1 — Use `•`, never `-`.** Every bullet starts with the dot symbol `•` followed by a space. Do **not** use a hyphen/dash (`-`) as a bullet marker, because a leading `-` on a line that also contains numbers or variables (which is most lines in this format) is indistinguishable from a negative sign. This is a hard rule with no exceptions — check every note before finalizing.

```
✅ CORRECT:
note:"• v_avg = average speed (m/s)
• d = total distance (m)
• t = total time (s)"

❌ WRONG (dash looks like a negative sign):
note:"- v_avg = average speed (m/s)
- d = total distance (m)
- t = total time (s)"
```

**Rule 2 — One fact per bullet.** Don't chain multiple facts onto one bullet with commas or semicolons where they could instead be split into their own bullets. If a bullet needs its own sub-detail, indent a nested `•` bullet directly beneath it with a couple of spaces of indent.

**Rule 3 — A one-line scene-setting sentence is still allowed before the bullets, not instead of them.** A note may open with a single short plain sentence introducing the node (e.g. "Describes how population grows when resources are unlimited.") — but everything after that opening line, including variable lists, examples, calculations, and explanations, must be bulleted.

**Rule 4 — Real line breaks between bullets, never `\n`.** This follows the existing line-break rule elsewhere in this document: press shift+enter / use an actual newline between each `•` bullet, never the literal text `\n`.

**Rule 5 — Headings inside a note (e.g. "Variables:", "Calculation:", "Check:") stay as plain short lines, not bullets themselves** — they introduce the bullet group that follows them.

**Rule 6 — Use simple, everyday words inside every bullet.** Simplification is not just shape (bullets instead of paragraphs) — it is also word choice. Write each bullet the way you'd explain it out loud to someone who is new to the topic, not the way a textbook glossary would phrase it.
- Prefer short, common, plain-English words over long or academic ones: "makes" not "facilitates," "shows" not "demonstrates," "speeds up" not "accelerates" (unless the technical term itself is the thing being taught, e.g. defining "acceleration" as a variable).
- Keep sentences short. One idea per bullet, said plainly.
- Technical terms, symbols, and field-specific vocabulary (variable names, named laws, chemical names, etc.) still belong when they are the actual subject of the node — simplify the surrounding explanation, not the correct terminology itself.
- If a genuinely technical word is unavoidable, follow it with a short plain-word clarification in parentheses the first time it appears in that note.

```
✅ CORRECT (simple words):
note:"• Enzymes speed up reactions in the body
• They work best at a certain temperature and pH
• Too much heat can break (denature) an enzyme so it stops working"

❌ WRONG (needlessly complex words):
note:"• Enzymes facilitate biochemical reactions within organisms
• They exhibit optimal catalytic efficacy at specific thermal and pH parameters
• Excessive thermal exposure induces denaturation, abrogating catalytic function"
```

```
✅ CORRECT full example:
note:"A car travels 150 km in 2 hours.

Variables:
• d = distance traveled (150 km)
• t = time taken (2 h)
• v_avg = average speed (unknown, solve for this)

Calculation:
• v_avg = d / t
• v_avg = 150 / 2
• v_avg = 75 km/h

Check:
• Units: km ÷ h = km/h ✓
• 75 km/h is a reasonable highway driving speed ✓"
```

**Rule 7 — Notes must be detailed, not shallow. Bullet format is necessary but not sufficient.** A note made of 3–5 short, generic, label-like bullets is NOT acceptable even though it is correctly bulleted. Every leaf note must cover as many of the following facets as genuinely apply to that node:
- **What it is** — a clear identity/definition (the opening sentence from Rule 3 can cover this).
- **How/why it works** — the mechanism, process, or reasoning, not just the bare fact.
- **Specific numbers, names, or details** — exact figures, counts, units, named parts, sub-types — concrete detail, not vague generalities.
- **Significance** — why it matters, what it affects, or what depends on it.
- **A concrete example, exception, or edge case**, when one exists.

As a floor, a leaf node covering a real fact or concept needs **at least 6–8 bullets** spread across these facets (nested sub-bullets count toward this). If a node genuinely can't support that many, that's usually a sign it's too broad and should be split into child nodes (see Rule 11 on splitting examples into siblings) — not a sign that a thin note is acceptable.

```
❌ TOO SHALLOW (bulleted correctly, but not detailed — this is what to avoid):
note:"• Carries oxygen
• In red blood cells
• Four subunits
• Each subunit carries one O₂
• Also carries some CO₂"

✅ DETAILED (same node, properly filled out across multiple facets):
note:"A protein in red blood cells that carries gases through the blood.

What it is:
• A protein made of 4 folded chains (subunits) — 2 alpha, 2 beta
• Each subunit holds one heme group with an iron atom at its center

How it works:
• The iron atom grabs onto one oxygen molecule (O₂)
• So one hemoglobin can carry up to 4 O₂ at once
• Picks up O₂ in the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful
• Releases O₂ in body tissues, where oxygen is scarce (low O₂ there)

Extra roles:
• Carries some carbon dioxide (CO₂) back to the lungs to be breathed out
• Helps buffer (steady) the blood's pH

Why it matters:
• Low hemoglobin (anemia) means tissues don't get enough oxygen
• Normal range is roughly 12–17 g/dL, depending on sex and age"
```

### THE FILLING MANDATE — FILL AND EXPAND, NEVER REMOVE, NEVER STOP EARLY

This prompt's only job, on every single run, is to **fill and expand**. It is never to remove, shrink, condense, or summarize away existing content, and it never stops partway through a topic just because a reasonable amount has already been produced.

- **If the user gives a topic, subject, field, or chapter and there is no existing mind map to expand:** the AI does not ask the user to supply one first. The AI generates the complete structure and content itself, from its own knowledge of that topic, exactly as if a human had handed it a finished outline to convert. Silence about existing material is not a reason to produce a shallow map — it is a reason to build the whole thing.
- **If the user gives an existing mind map to expand:** every existing node, path, and piece of content stays. Nothing is deleted, reworded-down, or compressed to make room for new content. New nodes are added as additional siblings/children; existing notes are only ever added to, not trimmed.
- **Filling does not stop until the topic is actually exhausted at the required depth** (see the Depth Checklist and Rule 7 in YOUR TASK below) — not until the output "feels long enough." If a branch of the topic has more sub-aspects, worked examples, edge cases, or real-world applications left to cover, keep going.
- This mandate has the same zero-tolerance weight as the Equation Accuracy Mandate above: an output that stops early, removes something, or leaves a branch thin is a failed output, not an acceptable partial one.

## OUTPUT RULES (STRICT)

1. **Every line is one topic node.**
2. **The FIRST line MUST be `lvl1[1]Title`** — this becomes the root (central) topic. There is exactly ONE root.
3. **Use `lvlN[I]` to define hierarchy depth and sibling order:**
   - `N` = level depth (1 = root, 2 = main branches, 3 = sub-branches, 4, 5, etc.)
   - `I` = sibling index within that level (1, 2, 3...)
4. **Parent-child relationship:** A node at `lvlN` is a child of the **most recent** node at `lvlN-1`.
5. **Hierarchy must increase or stay the same — never skip levels going down:**
   - ✅ `lvl1[1]` → `lvl2[1]` → `lvl3[1]` (correct: +1 each step)
   - ✅ `lvl1[1]` → `lvl2[1]` → `lvl2[2]` (correct: sibling at same level)
   - ✅ `lvl3[2]` → `lvl2[3]` (correct: moving back up to add a new branch)
   - ❌ `lvl1[1]` → `lvl3[1]` (WRONG: skipped lvl2)
   - ❌ `lvl2[1]` → `lvl4[1]` (WRONG: skipped lvl3)
6. **Index `[I]` must increase for siblings at the same level. Never repeat the same `lvlN[I]` pair:**
   - ✅ `lvl2[1]Apple` then `lvl2[2]Banana` then `lvl2[3]Cherry`
   - ❌ `lvl2[1]Apple` then `lvl2[1]Banana` (WRONG: same index repeated)
7. **Optional inline overrides** go inside `{ }` after the title.
8. **The heading/title (the text after the hierarchical address and before the `{...}`) MUST be a single line of plain text.** Do NOT insert line breaks into the title itself. Only the content inside the `note` field may contain multiple real line breaks (as needed for explanations, examples, or structured lists).

## HIERARCHICAL ADDRESS SYSTEM (CRITICAL — READ CAREFULLY)

**Every line is a PATH from the root to that specific node.** Not a single coordinate, but a cumulative address that shows the complete route.

### Understanding the PATH

Think of it like directions: "Go to Europe → Go to France → Go to Paris → Go to the Louvre."

Each `lvlN[I]` is one step in that path. You **never repeat a step**; you only add new steps going down, or remove steps going back up.

**Path Example:**
```
lvl1[1]                           ← Root: Europe
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]                    ← One step down: Europe → France
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]            ← Two steps down: Europe → France → Paris
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]    ← Three steps down: Europe → France → Paris → Louvre
```

### What is `lvlN[I]`?

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `N` | **Depth** — how many steps from root (1=root, 2=one step down, 3=two steps down, etc.) |
| `[I]` | **Birth order** — which sibling among those at this depth under the same parent (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) |

### CRITICAL RULE: The Path ACCUMULATES

**Every line contains the FULL path from root to that node.**

**WRONG (incomplete path):**
```
❌ lvl3[1]Orange                  ← This looks like depth 3, but HOW did we get here?
```

**CORRECT (complete path):**
```
✅ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Orange   ← Root → Level 2 → Level 3 = Orange
```

### Example: Citrus Tree (Complete Path)

```
lvl1[1]Citrus                           ← Root (depth 1, sibling 1)

lvl1[1]lvl2[1]Sweet                     ← Depth 2, sibling 1 under Citrus
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Orange            ← Depth 3, sibling 1 under Sweet
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]Navel      ← Depth 4, sibling 1 under Orange
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[2]Valencia   ← Depth 4, sibling 2 under Orange (sister of Navel)
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]Mandarin          ← Depth 3, sibling 2 under Sweet (sister of Orange)

lvl1[1]lvl2[2]Sour                      ← Depth 2, sibling 2 under Citrus (sister of Sweet)
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[1]Lemon             ← Depth 3, sibling 1 under Sour
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[2]Lime              ← Depth 3, sibling 2 under Sour (sister of Lemon)
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[3]Grapefruit        ← Depth 3, sibling 3 under Sour
```

**Visual tree (same data):**
```
Citrus (root)
├── Sweet (2nd level, 1st sibling)
│   ├── Orange (3rd level, 1st sibling)
│   │   ├── Navel (4th level, 1st sibling)
│   │   └── Valencia (4th level, 2nd sibling)
│   └── Mandarin (3rd level, 2nd sibling)
└── Sour (2nd level, 2nd sibling)
    ├── Lemon (3rd level, 1st sibling)
    ├── Lime (3rd level, 2nd sibling)
    └── Grapefruit (3rd level, 3rd sibling)
```

### RULE 1: Each Level Increases by Exactly 1

When you go deeper, add ONE new `lvlN[I]` pair. **Never skip.**

**CORRECT:**
```
✅ lvl1[1]
✅ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]                ← added lvl2[1]
✅ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]         ← added lvl3[1]
✅ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]  ← added lvl4[1]
```

**WRONG (skipped lvl3):**
```
❌ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl4[1]         ← jumped from lvl2 to lvl4, skipped lvl3!
```

### RULE 2: Sibling Indices Reset Under a New Parent

Each parent has its own set of children, numbered 1, 2, 3...

**CORRECT:**
```
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]Branch A
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Sub A1      ← under Branch A: 1st child
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]Sub A2      ← under Branch A: 2nd child
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]Branch B           ← back up, new sibling of Branch A
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[1]Sub B1      ← under Branch B: 1st child (resets to [1]!)
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[2]Sub B2      ← under Branch B: 2nd child
```

Notice: `lvl3[1]` appears TWICE, but under different parents (Branch A vs. Branch B). This is OK because the full paths are different:
- `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]` (under Branch A)
- `lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[1]` (under Branch B)

**WRONG (repeating parent sibling):**
```
❌ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]Branch A
❌ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]Branch B         ← WRONG! lvl2[1] was already used. This should be lvl2[2].
```

### RULE 3: Never Repeat the Same `lvlN[I]` Pair in the Same Line

**WRONG (repeated lvl2[1]):**
```
❌ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl2[1]Something
                      ↑↑↑↑↑
                  This lvl2[1] appears twice!
```

The parser will see the LAST `lvl2[1]` and ignore the earlier one. The node becomes a child of lvl1[1] at depth 2, not depth 4.

**CORRECT:**
```
✅ lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]Something
```

### RULE 4: When Moving UP (Adding a Sibling), Truncate and Increment

To add a sibling at a higher level, remove the lower levels and increment the index.

**CORRECT:**
```
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]Sub A1 (deep node)
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[2]Sub A2 (sibling of above) ← just change last [1] to [2]
lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]Sub B        (sibling at lvl3)  ← remove lvl4, change [1] to [2]
lvl1[1]lvl2[2]Branch C            (sibling at lvl2)  ← remove lvl3 and lvl4, change [1] to [2]
```

### PATH VERIFICATION CHECKLIST (Before Every Line)

Before you write a line, verify ALL of these:

1. **Count `lvl` markers:** A depth-5 node must have exactly 5 `lvl` markers.
   ```
   lvl1[1]lvl2[x]lvl3[x]lvl4[x]lvl5[x]  ← 5 markers = depth 5 ✓
   lvl1[1]lvl2[x]lvl3[x]lvl4[x]         ← 4 markers = depth 4 ✓
   lvl1[1]lvl2[x]lvl3[x]lvl3[x]         ← repeat lvl3! ✗ WRONG
   ```

2. **Check levels increase:** 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 (never backward, never jump).
   ```
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]        ✓ correct
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl2[1]        ✗ goes backward
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl4[1]               ✗ skips lvl3
   ```

3. **Check no duplicate lvlN pairs:** Each `lvlN` appears exactly once per line.
   ```
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]lvl4[1]        ✓ all unique
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl2[2]        ✗ lvl2 appears twice
   ```

4. **Verify the LAST marker determines depth:** Everything before it is parent context.
   ```
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl4[2]MyTopic ← last marker is lvl4[2], so depth = 4 ✓
   ```

5. **Check index uniqueness among siblings:** No two lines with the same full path ending.
   ```
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Topic A
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Topic B        ✗ WRONG: same path lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]
   
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]Topic A
   lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]Topic B        ✓ CORRECT: different indices [1] vs [2]
   ```

### Common MISTAKES (DESTRUCTIVE)

| Mistake | Why It's Wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `lvl1[1]lvl3[1]X` | Skipped lvl2 | `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]X` |
| `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[1]lvl3[2]Y` | Repeated lvl3 | `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]lvl3[2]Y` |
| `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]A` then `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]B` | Same path twice | `lvl1[1]lvl2[1]A` then `lvl1[1]lvl2[2]B` |
| `lvl1[1]lvl2[3]lvl4[1]Z` | Skipped lvl3 | `lvl1[1]lvl2[3]lvl3[1]lvl4[1]Z` |
| `lvl2[1]Root` | Missing lvl1 | `lvl1[1]Root` |
| `lvl1[1]lvl2[2]lvl3[1]lvl2[3]W` | lvl2 appears twice | `lvl1[1]lvl2[3]lvl3[1]W` |

## ⛔ CRITICAL WARNINGS — AI MUST FOLLOW THESE

### WARNING 1: Line Breaks in Notes — NO `\n` ESCAPES

**The `\n` character is FORBIDDEN in notes.** It will not render properly.

**WRONG (do NOT do this):**
```
note:"Variables:\nv_avg = average speed (m/s)\nd = total distance (m)\n\nExamples:\n1. Car..."
```

**CORRECT (use real line breaks):**
```
note:"Variables:
• v_avg = average speed (m/s)
• d = total distance (m)

Examples:
1. Car..."
```

When writing notes, press **shift+enter** to create a real line break, not the text `\n`.

### WARNING 2: Never Cram Examples into One Node

**WRONG (all examples in one note):**
```
lvl5[1]Formula{equation:"...", note:"Variables: ...\n\nExamples:\n1. Car → ...\n2. Runner → ...\n3. Bike → ...\n4. Train → ..."}
```

**CORRECT (each example is a sibling node, and each note is bulleted per the Note Format Mandate):**
```
lvl5[1]Formula{equation:"...", note:"Variables:
• v_avg = average speed (m/s)
• d = total distance (m)"}

lvl5[2]Example 1: Car{note:"A car travels 150 km in 2 hours.

Calculation:
• v_avg = 150 / 2
• v_avg = 75 km/h

Check:
• Units correct (km ÷ h = km/h)"}

lvl5[3]Example 2: Runner{note:"A runner covers 400 m in 50 seconds.

Calculation:
• v_avg = 400 / 50
• v_avg = 8 m/s

Check:
• Standard SI units, value is a realistic running speed"}

lvl5[4]Example 3: Bicycle{note:"..."}

lvl5[5]Example 4: Train{note:"..."}
```

**Why?** 
• Cleaner hierarchy — each example stands alone
• No line breaks needed inside a single example's note
• User can collapse/expand examples individually
• Each example can have its own calculation steps on separate bulleted lines

### WARNING 3: Variable Definitions Must Be Multi-Line and Bulleted

**WRONG (all on one line):**
```
note:"Variables: v_avg = average speed (m/s), d = total distance (m), t = total time (s)"
```

**WRONG (multi-line but no bullets):**
```
note:"Variables:

v_avg = average speed (m/s)
d = total distance (m)
t = total time (s)"
```

**CORRECT (real line breaks AND a `•` bullet on each variable — never a `-` dash):**
```
note:"Variables:
• v_avg = average speed (m/s)
• d = total distance (m)
• t = total time (s)"
```

### WARNING 4: Each Example Calculation Is Multiple Bulleted Lines

**WRONG (calculation squashed into one line):**
```
note:"Car travels 150 km in 2 h. v_avg = 150/2 = 75 km/h. Units: distance/time."
```

**CORRECT (calculation steps are separate bulleted lines):**
```
note:"A car travels 150 km in 2 hours.

Calculation:
• v_avg = 150 / 2
• v_avg = 75 km/h

Check:
• Units are distance/time, correct"
```

## ⛔ NOTE, TITLE, AND LABEL FIELD SAFETY (CRITICAL — NEW)

These failures have nothing to do with equations — they come from how the parser reads the plain text of `note`, the title, and `label`. They are just as capable of silently corrupting the output, and because a broken split can swallow every entry that comes after it, they are treated with the same zero-tolerance seriousness as the equation rules above.

### WARNING 5: An Unescaped `"` Inside `note` Truncates the Note Early

The parser reads a `note:"..."` value by scanning forward until it hits the *next* unescaped double-quote character. Whatever comes after that quote is treated as outside the note — at best you silently lose the rest of your note text, at worst the leftover text corrupts the parsing of the rest of that line's `{ }` block.

**WRONG (the note will be cut off right after "high"):**
```
note:"The general's strategy was described as "high risk, high reward" by analysts."
```
This note is silently truncated to just `The general's strategy was described as ` — everything from the first embedded `"` onward, including the closing `}` of the block, is lost or misread.

**CORRECT — do ONE of the following:**
- Avoid straight double-quotes in note text entirely; use single quotes, or typographic/curly quotes (`'` `'` `"` `"`) instead — these are NOT the delimiter character and are always safe:
  ```
  note:"The general's strategy was described as "high risk, high reward" by analysts."
  ```
  (using curly quotes `" "` instead of straight `" "` around the quoted phrase)
- Or, if you must use a straight double-quote, escape it with a backslash immediately before it: `\"`. The parser strips a `\"` back down to a plain `"` when it reads the note, so this is safe:
  ```
  note:"The general's strategy was described as \"high risk, high reward\" by analysts."
  ```

**Never** write an unescaped `"` inside a `note`, a `label`, or a title. Titles and labels have even less tolerance for this than notes (they use a plainer match with no escape handling at all), so avoid straight double-quotes in title text altogether — use curly quotes or rephrase without quotation marks.

### WARNING 6: An Unbalanced `{` or `}` Anywhere in Title or Note Text Corrupts Entry Splitting

The parser figures out where one line/entry ends and the next begins by counting `{` and `}` characters across the **entire pasted output**, not just within one line's own `{ }` override block. It only treats a newline as the end of an entry when the running brace-count is back to zero. This means a single stray, unmatched curly brace anywhere in a title or note — even one that has nothing to do with the override syntax — throws off entry boundaries for every line that follows, until (if ever) the count happens to rebalance.

**WRONG (the lone `{` before "special" is never closed):**
```
lvl3[2]Edge Case{note:"Only applies when {special conditions apply."}
```
Because that embedded `{` inside the note text is never matched by a `}`, the parser thinks the current entry's brace block is still open, and will silently absorb some number of the following lines into what it thinks is still this one note, corrupting the rest of the document.

**CORRECT:** Never use a bare, unmatched `{` or `}` in title or note text. If you need curly braces for their own sake in prose (rare, but e.g. describing set notation in words, or code-like text), always keep them paired within the same field: `{special conditions}` is fine (it opens and closes), a lone `{` or `}` alone is not. When in doubt, rewrite using parentheses or square brackets in prose instead of curly braces — they carry no special meaning to this parser.

### Field Safety Checklist (run this alongside the Equation Verification Checklist)

Before finalizing, for every `note`, title, and `label` in the entire document:
- ✅ No unescaped straight double-quote (`"`) appears inside any `note` value (curly quotes or an escaped `\"` only)
- ✅ No straight double-quote appears in any title or `label` value at all
- ✅ Every `{` in a title or note has a matching `}` within that same field, and vice versa — count them
- ✅ No stray literal `{` or `}` was left over from drafting (e.g. copy-pasted placeholder syntax)

## INLINE OVERRIDE SYNTAX (inside `{ }`)

Multiple overrides can be combined with commas:
```
{equation:"E = mc^2", eq_w:280, eq_h:70, eq_font:40, note:"Details here", label:"Process"}
```

**⛔ Field order matters whenever `equation` is present: `equation` (plus its `eq_w`/`eq_h`/`eq_font` sizing) always comes first, `note` always comes last.** Never write `note` before `equation` inside the `{ }` — the example above is the only correct order for any Equage node. `label`, if used, goes after `note`. For non-Equage nodes (no `equation` field), `note` and `label` may appear in either order since there's no equation to sequence around.

### Full Override Reference Table

| Key | Description | Example | When to Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| `note` | Attached note text (supports line breaks) | `note:"Line 1\nLine 2"` | **REQUIRED** on every node |
| `label` | Single-word tag for categorization | `label:"Concept"` | Only when adding classification value |
| `equation` | LaTeX equation rendered as **Equage** (image) | `equation:"v = \\frac{d}{t}"` | For formulas, equations, relationships, derivations |
| `eq_w` | Equage image width (px) | `eq_w:260` | Size the equation image |
| `eq_h` | Equage image height (px) | `eq_h:70` | Size the equation image |
| `eq_font` | Font size for equage text | `eq_font:35` | Adjust text size in equation image |

**NOTE:** Do NOT use `fill`, `text`, `border`, or `line` for equation/formula nodes. These color overrides are removed for Equage-based workflows. The exporter renders Equage as self-contained images with their own styling.

## EQUAGE — EQUATION IMAGE RENDERING

**Equage** = visual representation of equations, formulas, mathematical relationships, derivations, or symbolic expressions as rendered PNG/JPG images.

### When to Use Equage — ⛔ MANDATORY, NOT OPTIONAL

**If it applies, you use it. This is not a stylistic choice.** Equage MUST be used — a real `equation:"..."` field, not a sentence in a `note` that just describes the formula in words — for ANY node whose core subject is:
- A mathematical formula or equation (e.g., kinetic energy, quadratic formula)
- A derivation or multi-step algebraic proof
- A symbolic relationship or proportional link (even in non-math fields like biology: "trait A ∝ trait B")
- A calculable procedure or constraint
- Any other structured symbolic expression

**Do not describe a formula in prose or bullets instead of rendering it.** Writing `• Speed equals distance divided by time` in a note where `equation:"v = \\\\frac{d}{t}"` belongs is a failure of this rule, even though the fact stated is correct — the symbolic relationship itself must appear as a real Equage image, with the plain-word explanation living alongside it in the `note`, not replacing it.

**⛔ The reverse mistake is just as broken: never write the formula a second time as a bullet in `note` once it already has a real `equation:"..."` field, and never let a bullet marker or a "Equation:" label leak into the `equation:"..."` string itself.** A node like this has three stacked failures:
```
{note:"...\n\nEquation:\n• F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2", equation:"• F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2", eq_font:50}
```
(1) `note` is written before `equation` — the wrong field order (see "Field order matters" in Inline Override Syntax above; `equation` always comes first). (2) The formula is redundantly restated as a plain bullet inside `note` under an "Equation:" sub-heading — once a real `equation:"..."` field exists, `note` should hold only the variable definitions and plain-word explanation, never the raw formula again in any form. (3) The `equation:"..."` value itself is not valid LaTeX — it starts with a bullet character (`•`), which the renderer draws as a literal, meaningless dot in the equation image, and it uses a plain-text slash (`q_1 q_2 / r^2`) instead of the required `\\\\frac{}{}` command for division. The correct version of this node is:
```
{equation:"F = k \\\\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}", eq_w:260, eq_h:70, eq_font:35, note:"F = electrostatic force (N)\nk = Coulomb's constant (8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²)\nq₁, q₂ = the two charges (C)\nr = distance between the charges (m)\n\nOpposite charges attract; like charges repel. The force weakens with the square of the distance."}
```
The `equation:"..."` field is pure math and nothing else: no bullet characters, no "Equation:" or any other label text, no leading/trailing prose — just the LaTeX expression, exactly as the Supported LaTeX Commands section below defines it. Any division in an equation field is written as `\\\\frac{numerator}{denominator}`, never as a bare `/`.

**Scan every branch as you go, not just where the topic is explicitly "math."** Formulas and symbolic relationships hide inside non-math topics constantly — a rate, a ratio, a conversion, a law named after someone, a proportionality, a threshold condition, a growth/decay pattern. Whenever a fact you're about to write down is genuinely a symbolic relationship between quantities (not just a number or a label), it gets its own Equage node — either the node itself if the relationship is its core subject, or a dedicated child node if it comes up inside a broader topic.

Equage is **field-agnostic**. It works equally well in:
- **Math/Physics/Chemistry:** kinematic equations, thermodynamics, reaction rates
- **Biology:** population growth models, metabolic pathways, allele frequency equations
- **Economics:** supply-demand curves, elasticity formulas, present value calculations
- **Engineering:** stress-strain relationships, control system transfer functions
- **Any field:** whenever a relationship or rule is best expressed symbolically

### Equage Syntax

```
lvl3[1]Node Title{equation:"LaTeX here", eq_w:280, eq_h:80, eq_font:35, note:"Variable explanations here"}
```

**Three required properties for Equage nodes:**

1. **`equation:"LaTeX"`** — The symbolic expression, using only supported LaTeX commands (see below). Nothing else lives inside this string: no bullet characters, no "Equation:" labels, no bare `/` for division (use `\\frac{}{}`).
2. **`eq_w`, `eq_h`, `eq_font`** — Sizing parameters (see guidelines below). All three are required together — never emit `eq_font` alone without `eq_w` and `eq_h`, or vice versa.
3. **`note` with variable definitions** — MUST include definitions of all variables in the equation, and MUST NOT restate the formula itself as a bullet — once `equation:"..."` exists, the raw formula does not appear a second time anywhere in `note`.

### Supported LaTeX Commands for Equage

The exporter uses Matplotlib's mathtext engine. **You MUST restrict all equations to only these commands:**

**⛔ THE BACKSLASH-DOUBLING RULE (explicit — read this before writing any equation):** Every command name in the list below is written with a single backslash (e.g. `\frac`, `\cos`, `\theta`) because that's the real LaTeX command name. But when you type the actual `equation:"..."` text for this hierarchy format, **you must type every one of those backslashes TWICE.** Write `\\frac`, `\\cos`, `\\theta` — not `\frac`, `\cos`, `\theta`. Every worked example in this document already follows this convention (look at any `equation:"..."` value above or below — they all use double backslashes); this is now stated explicitly so it is never missed. The one exception where the count is different, not the same: a matrix/`cases`/`array` row break is itself two real LaTeX backslash characters (`\\`), so doubled it becomes **four** backslash characters (`\\\\`) — see the Matrix-Like Environments section above for worked examples. Getting this wrong (e.g. writing a single backslash for a command, or two backslashes where a row break needed four) is one of the most common sources of a broken or garbled equation image, so double-check every backslash count before finalizing.

**Greek letters (lowercase):** \alpha \beta \gamma \delta \epsilon \zeta \eta \theta \iota \kappa \lambda \mu \nu \xi \pi \rho \sigma \tau \upsilon \phi \chi \psi \omega
*(Do NOT use \omicron — write `o` instead)*

**Greek letters (uppercase):** \Delta \Gamma \Lambda \Omega \Pi \Psi \Sigma \Theta \Xi

**Greek variants:** \varepsilon \varphi \vartheta \varpi \varrho \varsigma

**Subscripts / Superscripts:** `x_1`, `x^2`, `x_{12}`, `x^{2n}`, `x_{i,j}`

**Fractions:** `\frac{a}{b}`

**Derivatives:** `\frac{d}{dx}`, `\partial`

**Integrals:** `\int`, `\int_0^\infty`

**Sums & Products:** `\sum_{i=1}^n`, `\prod_{i=1}^n`

**Square roots:** `\sqrt{}`, `\sqrt[3]{x}`

**Limits:** `\lim_{x \to 0}`

**Vectors:** `\vec{v}`, `\mathbf{v}`

**Operators:** `\cdot`, `\times`, `\pm`, `\mp`, `\leq`, `\geq`, `\neq`, `\approx`, `\propto`

**Accents:** `\bar{x}`, `\dot{x}`, `\ddot{x}`, `\hat{x}`, `\tilde{x}`

**Spacing:** `\,` (thin), `\;` (medium), `\quad`, `\qquad` — **prefer a plain space over `\,`/`\;` wherever possible** (e.g. write `v \, dt` as `v\ dt` or even just `v dt`); see the failure pattern below for why.

**Parentheses:** `\left(`, `\right)`, `\left[`, `\right]`, `\left\{`, `\right\}` *(must match in pairs — every `\left` needs exactly one corresponding `\right`, no exceptions)*

**Font styles:** `\mathrm{...}` (roman), `\mathbf{...}` (bold), `\mathsf{...}` (sans-serif), `\mathtt{...}` (typewriter)

**Other:** `\hbar`, `\nabla`, `\infty`, `\cdots`, `\vdots`, `\ddots`, `\rightarrow`, `\leftarrow`

### ⛔ Real Failure Patterns Observed — Match These EXACTLY, No Exceptions

These are not theoretical — every one of them has been observed corrupting or destroying real generated equations. Check every equation you write against this list before moving on:

| Pattern | Why it fails | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| A bare comma (`,`) anywhere in the equation that is not deliberately meant to start a new display line | The exporter splits equations into multiple stacked lines on top-level commas. A comma you didn't intend as a separator (e.g. inside a casual aside) will silently cut your equation in half. | If you need a literal comma in the math (e.g. `x_{i,j}`, or a coordinate pair), make sure it is inside `{ }`, `( )`, or `[ ]` — never a bare, ungrouped comma. |
| `\,` or `\;` immediately before a differential, e.g. `\int v \, dt` | The comma inside `\,`/`\;` can be misread as a line-separator, severing the differential (`dt`, `dx`, `dv`, ...) from the rest of the equation, leaving a dangling `\` with nothing after it. | Use a plain space instead: `\int v\ dt` or `\int v dt`. Never rely on `\,`/`\;` directly before a differential. |
| A trailing/orphaned backslash anywhere, i.e. a `\` with no valid command name immediately after it | A lone `\` is not valid LaTeX under any circumstance and will fail to parse — this is always the sign of a cut-off or malformed command. | Re-read every `\` in the equation and confirm it is immediately followed by a real command name from the supported list. If it isn't, fix it before finalizing. |
| `\(`, `\)`, `\[`, `\]` used as delimiters around the equation or part of it | These are LaTeX's own inline/display math delimiters. The `equation:"..."` field IS already the math body — it needs no delimiters at all. These have no meaning to the renderer and will break parsing. | Never include `\(`, `\)`, `\[`, or `\]` anywhere in the equation field. Just write the bare math content. |
| A bullet character (`•`) or a label like `Equation:` copied into the `equation:"..."` value (e.g. `equation:"• F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2"`) | Note-formatting characters aren't LaTeX. The renderer draws the bullet as a stray, meaningless dot (or fails outright) instead of parsing it as math. | The `equation:"..."` value is pure math and nothing else — no `•`, no "Equation:" label, no surrounding prose. Move any such text out; it does not belong inside the quotes. |
| A bare `/` used for division inside an equation (e.g. `q_1 q_2 / r^2` instead of a proper fraction) | Matplotlib's mathtext renders a raw slash flat and cramped instead of a real fraction bar, which also signals the equation wasn't written with the required LaTeX commands. | Always use `\\frac{numerator}{denominator}` for division — e.g. `k \\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}` — never a bare `/`. |
| Inventing a `\command` that is not in the Supported LaTeX Commands list above (e.g. writing `\X`, `\Y`, `\=`, or putting a stray backslash before an ordinary variable like `\x`) | Only the commands explicitly listed are guaranteed to exist in the renderer's vocabulary. An invented or misremembered command fails immediately with an "unknown symbol" error. | Never write a backslash before a plain variable name or a symbol like `=` — those never need one. If a symbol you want isn't on the supported list, don't guess; use a supported alias, wrap it in `\mathrm{}`, or spell it out in the `note` instead. |
| Unbalanced `{ }`, `( )`, `[ ]`, or unmatched `\left`/`\right` pairs | Every opening bracket/delimiter needs its matching close, or the whole equation fails to parse. | Before finalizing, literally count opens vs. closes of each bracket type in the equation — they must be equal. |

**Now safely usable (auto-normalized, content preserved):** `\overset{above}{base}`, `\underset{below}{base}` (both arguments now render — see below), `\big`/`\Big`/`\bigg`/`\Bigg` (followed by a delimiter), `\text{...}`, `\dfrac`/`\tfrac`/`\cfrac`, `\textbf`/`\bm`/`\boldsymbol`, `\operatorname{...}`, `\ge`/`\le`/`\ne`/`\to`/`\iff`/`\implies`/`\land`/`\lor`, `\arccot`/`\arcsec`/`\arccsc`, `\overrightarrow`, `\surd`, `\mathring`, `\qquad`, `\middle`, `\choose` (converted to `\binom`), `\Box`/`\square`/`\blacksquare`/`\Diamond`/`\lozenge`/`\bigstar`/`\checkmark`. The exporter's normalizer will clean these up automatically, so you no longer need to avoid them — see the caveats below for the one command that still loses information.

**Now safely usable with their OWN dedicated renderer (draw correctly, not mathtext-native but hand-drawn):** `\boxed{expr}` (box drawn around it), `\underline{expr}` (line drawn under it), `\cancel{expr}` / `\bcancel{expr}` (strike-through drawn over it), `\cancelto{value}{expr}` (strike-through with the value labeled), `\overbrace{expr}^{label}` / `\underbrace{expr}_{label}` (brace drawn with its label — the label is NOT dropped, unlike `\stackrel`). Use these freely; the label/argument you give them is preserved and shown.

### Commands That Are "Safe" But Lose Information (use with care)

Only one command family still drops part of its input — plan your equation so the dropped part isn't essential:

| Command | What actually gets shown | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| `\stackrel{above}{base}` | Only `base` (2nd arg) is rendered; `above` is silently dropped. (`\overset` and `\underset` are NOT in this category anymore — both of their arguments render correctly.) | Put the dropped annotation in words in the `note` field, e.g. `\stackrel{\Delta}{=}` renders as plain `=` — mention "≙ means defined-as" in the note. If you need the annotation to actually appear in the image, use `\overset{above}{base}` instead — it will render both parts. |
| `\color{name}{expr}`, `\textcolor{name}{expr}`, `\colorbox{name}{expr}` | Only `expr` (2nd arg) is rendered in the default text color; the color itself is always discarded | Never rely on color to convey meaning — say it in the note instead |
| `\big`/`\Big`/`\bigg`/`\Bigg` | The delimiter itself renders at normal size (sizing is stripped, not the symbol) | Fine to use for readability, but don't rely on it to visually distinguish nesting depth |

### Matrix-Like Environments — NOW FULLY SUPPORTED (real grid rendering, use them when they're the right tool)

**This section replaces the old "Commands to AVOID" table.** `pmatrix`, `bmatrix`, `vmatrix`, `Vmatrix`, `Bmatrix`, `matrix`, `smallmatrix`, `array`, `cases`, `aligned`, and `align` are drawn by a dedicated grid renderer: your actual content in every row/cell is preserved and shown, not discarded. Do NOT avoid these when the content genuinely is a matrix, a piecewise definition, or a multi-line aligned derivation — using one of these environments is now the *correct* choice for that content, and describing it only in the `note` instead is a downgrade, not a safety measure.

You MUST still follow this exact syntax, because getting it wrong silently produces a broken grid:

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Row separator | Rows are split on a `\\` (two-backslash) sequence that is NOT immediately followed by a letter. Given this format's backslash-doubling convention (see the dedicated section below), type a row break as **four** backslash characters in the equation field: `\\\\`. Never use a single backslash for a row break unless it is immediately followed by a space — that is tolerated as a typo-fallback but is not the intended syntax. |
| Cell separator | Cells within a row are split on a top-level `&`. Every row in the same environment should use the same number of `&`-separated cells (except `cases`, which is conventionally 2 cells per row: the value, then the condition). |
| `array` needs a column spec | `\begin{array}{cc}` (or `{rcl}`, etc.) — the `{cols}` argument right after `\begin{array}` is required; omitting it can break column layout. `matrix`/`pmatrix`/etc. do NOT take a column spec. |
| Delimiters are automatic per environment | `pmatrix` → `( )`, `bmatrix` → `[ ]`, `vmatrix` → `\| \|`, `Vmatrix` → `‖ ‖`, `Bmatrix` → `{ }`, `cases` → `{` only (no right delimiter, matching real LaTeX). `matrix`, `array`, and `smallmatrix` get **no** delimiter by default — wrap the whole block in `\left...\right` yourself if you want brackets around an `array`/`matrix`. Do not add your own `\begin{pmatrix}` content that also manually draws its own parens — that duplicates them. |
| `\hline` / `\cline{a-b}` | Real horizontal rules, only meaningful inside `array` (top-level, not nested inside a cell). `\cline{1-2}` draws a partial rule under columns 1–2. |
| `\multicolumn{n}{align}{content}` | Only meaningful inside `array`; spans `content` across `n` columns with alignment `align` (e.g. `c`). |
| `aligned`/`align` | Internally converted to `array{rcl}` and rendered the same way — write them exactly like a 3-column `array` (typically `expr & = & expr` per row), using a 4-backslash row break between rows (see the worked examples just below). |
| One node = one equation, still | Even though these environments now render correctly, keep each equation field to ONE self-contained expression. Don't mix an unrelated `note`-only explanation into the equation field. |

**Correct examples:**
```
equation:"\\begin{cases} x^2 & x \\ge 0 \\\\ -x^2 & x < 0 \\end{cases}"
```
```
equation:"\\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \\end{pmatrix}"
```
```
equation:"\\begin{array}{rcl} x + y & = & 5 \\\\ 2x - y & = & 1 \\end{array}"
```
(In each example above, count the backslashes: `\\` = 2 backslash characters, used before every ordinary command name like begin/end/ge, since this format doubles every real single backslash. `\\\\` = 4 backslash characters, used ONLY for a row break, since the real LaTeX row separator is itself 2 characters ("\\") and doubling that gives 4.)

For genuinely unrecognized environments not in this list (e.g. `split`, `gather`, `multline`, `subarray`, `eqnarray`) — these still fall back to the literal environment name as a placeholder word, with contents discarded. Never use them; there is no reason to, since `array`/`aligned` covers the same need.

### Commands That Are Simply Removed (safe, but add nothing)

| Command | Result | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| `\tag{...}`, `\label{...}` | Removed entirely, including their argument — cleanly, no leftover text | Don't bother using them; they have no visual effect. Put the label as plain text in the title or note instead |
| `\mathcal`, `\mathbb`, `\mathfrak` | Rendered as plain roman (`\mathrm`) — the distinctive font style is lost, but the letters themselves still show | Fine for a single symbol like `\mathbb{R}`; if the specific font matters, say so in the note (e.g. "R here denotes the real numbers") |
| `\omicron` | Not a valid symbol | Write `o` instead |
| `\longrightarrow`, `\leftrightarrow` | Auto-converted to `\rightarrow`, `\leftarrow` respectively (same arrow, shorter stem) | Fine to use either form — the exporter normalizes it either way |
| Empty `\frac{}{}`, `\sqrt{}` | Auto-fills the empty slot with `0` rather than crashing | Never leave an equation part empty — this is a content mistake, not a formatting choice; always fill in every argument |

### Example: Using `\underset` / `\stackrel` Correctly

Since only the base (2nd argument) renders, always spell out the dropped annotation in the note:

```
lvl4[1]Limit Definition{equation:"\underset{n \to \infty}{\lim} \frac{1}{n} = 0", eq_w:260, eq_h:70, eq_font:35, note:"The equation image shows just '\lim  1/n = 0' — the 'n → ∞' below the lim symbol does not render, so it is spelled out here.

• As n approaches infinity, 1/n approaches 0"}
```

### Rule 2: Sizing Guidelines

| Complexity | Example | eq_font | eq_w | eq_h |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very short (≤10 chars) | `F = ma` | 50 | 160 | 50 |
| Short (≤20) | `\vec{F} = m\vec{a}` | 45 | 200 | 60 |
| Medium (≤40) | `\frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}` | 35 | 280 | 80 |
| Long (≤80) | Schrödinger, Navier–Stokes | 30 | 420 | 95 |
| Very long (>80) | Damped driven harmonic oscillator | 26 | 520 | 100 |

**Rule:** Smaller `eq_font` for longer equations; increase `eq_w` to fit without crowding.

### Rule 3: Multi-Step Derivations

For derivations with multiple steps, create separate sibling nodes. Each node contains ONE step with ONE equation.

**Example: Quadratic Formula Derivation**

## ⛔ NODE TITLE DISCIPLINE — IMAGE-MATCHING (IFM) AWARENESS

**Why this matters:** after this map is exported, a separate automated step (the IFM/Automatic Search pipeline) walks the finished tree and fetches one picture per node by searching Google Images for that node's **title, exactly as you wrote it**. You are not running that search yourself, but every title you write becomes a search query someone else's tool depends on, so title quality here directly determines image accuracy later.

**How the search pipeline handles a repeated title (so you don't have to fake uniqueness yourself):** if a title is unique across the whole tree, it's searched as-is. If the same title appears on more than one node, the pipeline automatically prefixes it with that node's parent title to disambiguate (`Tuberculosis` + `Diagnosis` → searches "Tuberculosis Diagnosis"); if the parent title is *also* shared by the other node(s) with the same title, it keeps climbing to the grandparent, great-grandparent, and so on, until the combined phrase is unique or it runs out of ancestors. A node left with a blank title borrows its nearest named ancestor's title outright rather than being searched as nothing.

**What this means for how you write titles here:**

1. **Repeating the same archetype/category title across many unrelated branches is expected and fine — do not "fix" it yourself.** `Diagnosis`, `Treatment`, `Comparison`, `Mechanism of Action`, `Risk Factors`, `Signs and Symptoms`, etc. are *supposed* to recur once per disease/drug/organism node — that repetition is exactly the case the parent-prefix rescue exists for. **Never rename these into a pre-qualified form yourself** (e.g. do not write `Tuberculosis Diagnosis` as the title when the node is really `Diagnosis` nested under `Tuberculosis`) — that bakes the ancestor permanently into the label, duplicates what the pipeline already does automatically at search time, and produces a redundant, sentence-like title that breaks the "short label, one idea" naming standard used throughout this format.
2. **Never give two children of the same parent the identical title.** A repeated title only makes sense when it's on genuinely different nodes elsewhere in the tree (different parents) — two siblings with the exact same title under the exact same parent is always a drafting mistake (a leftover duplicate, or two facts that should have been named differently), never a valid case. Catch and fix these before finishing.
3. **Watch for titles that collide with an unrelated node's title *and* whose parent title also collides with that unrelated node's parent title** — that's the case the automatic rescue can't cleanly resolve in one step (it has to keep climbing further up the tree, or settle while still ambiguous). This usually signals the same underlying problem as #2 one level removed: either an accidental duplicate branch, or a leaf/category title that's too generic for the context it's in (e.g. two different sibling groups both using bare `Type 1` / `Type 2` instead of the actual type names). When you spot this pattern, give the colliding nodes more specific, concrete titles rather than leaving it for ancestor-prefixing to sort out.
4. **Name specific, factual leaf nodes concretely, not generically.** A leaf node that states an actual fact (a specific symptom, drug, organism, date, structure) should be named for that fact (`Night Sweats`, not `Symptom`) — concrete leaf names are naturally far less likely to collide with an unrelated node elsewhere in a large map, and they also make for a far more accurate, specific image search than a bare category word ever could. Reserve generic labels (`Overview`, `Definition`, `Types`, `Examples`) for genuine grouping/category nodes, where the recurrence-and-parent-prefix behavior in point 1 is exactly what's supposed to happen.

**Quick self-check before finishing:** for every title that appears more than once in the tree, confirm each occurrence sits under a **different, distinctly-named** ancestor chain (this is what makes the automatic parent-prefix rescue land on an accurate, distinct search per node) — and that no two of those occurrences are actually siblings under the same parent.
